Sampling Designs

Every design here picks rocks at random in some way — except the last one. The question is not whether randomness is used, but what it is applied to.

  • Simple random — every rock equally likely. Fair, but scattered all over the plot: you dig a great many holes.
  • Stratified — split the ground into the three depth layers first, then take a random sample from each, in proportion to its size. Guarantees every layer is represented.
  • Cluster — pick a few holes at random and weigh every rock you find in them. Far less digging. A hole runs top to bottom, so it contains all three layers — each one a small copy of the population, which is the condition under which cluster sampling does well.
  • Multistage — pick a few holes at random, then a random sample of rocks within each hole. Less digging and less weighing.
  • Convenience — just take what's near the surface. No randomness at all.

Run 500 of each design is where the lesson is. Four of the five centre on the truth; they differ in spread. Convenience doesn't centre on the truth at all, and no sample size fixes that.

The Holes dug and Rocks weighed figures are the point of cluster and multistage sampling: they buy a much cheaper sample.

The rule worth taking away: good strata are uniform inside and different from each other; good clusters are varied inside, each a small copy of the whole. They are opposites, and the same ground can give you either.

For instructors: choose More options to switch the clusters from deep holes to shallow pits. Pits sit at a single depth, so they are uniform inside — the way real clusters (city blocks, classrooms) usually are. Run 500 again and the spread jumps, which is the design effect textbooks refer to when they say cluster sampling costs precision. It is hidden by default because it is a second question, worth asking only once the four designs are straight.
