Why t* and not 1.96 — Help

A confidence interval for a mean is x̄ ± (multiplier) × (standard error). The honest margin is z*σ/√n — but σ is a fact about the population, and you do not have it. You have s, computed from the same sample that gave you x̄. This page is about what that substitution costs and how t* pays for it.

  • The axis is in true standard errors. Nothing on it depends on your sample, so all three margins can be laid against the same ruler and compared.
  • The shaded band is the middle (confidence)% of the sampling distribution — the sample means for which a correctly-sized interval reaches μ.
  • This sample’s s is a slider because s is a different number in every sample. Set it below σ and the second recipe falls short; set it above and the interval is longer than it needed to be.
  • Draw a sample takes n real observations, computes x̄ and s from them, and tallies which recipes captured μ. That tally is the actual argument — one sample proves nothing.

The three recipes

  • z*σ/√n — the right length, always, and unusable: it needs σ.
  • z*s/√n — usable, and too short on average. Not because s is biased (it barely is), but because s is variable: half the time it comes in low, and those are the samples whose interval misses. Its real coverage is 2 Ft(z*, n−1) − 1, below the advertised level at every n.
  • t*s/√n — usable, and right. t* is defined as the number that makes the long-run coverage come out exactly as advertised when the standard error is estimated.

What t* does and does not do

t* does not repair the s you happen to have. In a sample where s came in low, the t interval is still shorter than it should be; in a sample where s came in high, it is longer than it needed to be. What t* fixes is the rate: over all samples, exactly the advertised fraction capture μ. Drag the s slider to see one sample get it wrong in each direction, then draw a few hundred and watch the rate come right anyway.

Raise n and t* slides down toward z*: with a lot of data, s is a reliable stand-in for σ and the correction stops mattering. That is why the two recipes agree in large samples and why the distinction is a small-sample one.
