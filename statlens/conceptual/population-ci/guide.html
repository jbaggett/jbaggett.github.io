Instructor Guide: Confidence Intervals by Repeated Sampling

Companion notes for the StatLens conceptual demo — where a margin of error comes from, and why the bootstrap can stand in for it

Launch in Presentation Mode

Overview ~12 min

The same four steps as a bootstrap mean interval, with one swapped: every repetition draws a fresh sample from the population instead of resampling the one sample you have. Choose the population shape, set n, press +1000, and the sampling distribution of x̄ builds. Its middle 90 / 95 / 99% is shaded; the half-width of that shaded band is the margin of error; step 4 gives that margin to one sample and draws the interval.

Why this is not just the Sampling Distribution Lab. The Lab is about the spread of a statistic and how it responds to n. This page is about the interval: where its width comes from, and why the interval around x̄ succeeds exactly when x̄ was typical.

The point to be careful about

The shaded band in step 3 is NOT a confidence interval. It is the central range of sample means, and it is centred on μ — which in real life you do not know. Saying “95% confidence interval” over that band teaches the commonest CI error there is: that the interval is a range the statistic falls into, rather than a range built around the statistic. The page keeps them apart deliberately — step 3 says middle 95% of sample means and margin of error; the words “confidence interval” appear only in step 4, over a bar centred on x̄.

The equivalence to draw out loud:

|x̄ − μ| ≤ m   ⇔   μ is inside [x̄ ± m]

Left-hand side: x̄ landed in the shaded band. Right-hand side: the interval captured μ. One fact, read from either end — which is why the page reports both and they never disagree.

Running it

  1. Start on a skewed population. It makes the point that the sampling distribution becomes symmetric even when the population is not — worth noticing before anything else.
  2. Press +1 a few times. Each press redraws step 2 (a new sample) and drops one mean into step 3. Ask what step 3 would look like after a thousand.
  3. Press +1000. Compare SD of the x̄'s with σ/√n in the readout. They should agree closely — this is the standard error formula arriving as an observation rather than an assertion.
  4. Change the level to 99%. The samples stand; only the shaded middle widens. The margin grows, and in step 4 the interval gets longer. “More confidence costs width” becomes something students watch rather than something they are told.
  5. Change n. Everything clears — the sampling distribution depends on n, so the old means are not comparable. Rebuild at a larger n and watch the band and the interval both narrow.
Discussion question. The shaded middle in step 3 is centred on μ, but your interval in step 4 is centred on x̄. When does the interval reach μ? Answer: exactly when x̄ landed inside the shaded band — the two statements are the same distance read from opposite ends.

Where the bootstrap comes in

Step 3 is impossible in practice: one sample is all anyone gets. The bootstrap substitutes the sample for the population and resamples that, producing a distribution of roughly the same width, centred on x̄ rather than on μ. Running this page beside Bootstrap: One Mean with the same n makes the comparison concrete — same shape of picture, same width, different centre.

Why the Percentile CI Works puts both distributions on one axis and is the natural follow-on. CI Coverage then runs many intervals and counts how many capture μ, which is the claim the confidence level actually makes.
