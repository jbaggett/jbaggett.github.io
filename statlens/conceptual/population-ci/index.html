Confidence Intervals by Repeated Sampling — Help

The same four-step shape as a bootstrap mean interval, with one step swapped: every repetition draws a fresh sample from the population instead of resampling your one sample.

  • Step 1 is the whole population, with μ marked. You never have this in practice, which is the point of the exercise.
  • Step 2 is the most recent sample of n, with its mean.
  • Step 3 collects one mean per sample. The shaded middle is the central 90 / 95 / 99% of those means — it is centred on μ, and its half-width is the margin of error.
  • Step 4 takes the first sample you drew and gives it that margin: x ± m. That is a confidence interval.

Read step 3 carefully. The shaded range is not a confidence interval — it is the range of sample means, and it is centred on μ, which you would not know. It becomes an interval for μ only when you take its half-width over to one sample, which is step 4.

The argument. The interval x ± m reaches μ exactly when x landed within m of μ — that is, exactly when x is inside the shaded middle. So the intervals that work are the ones from the shaded samples, which is the stated percentage of them. That is what the confidence level counts.

What the bootstrap does about it. You cannot draw step 3, because you cannot sample the population again. The bootstrap resamples your sample instead, producing a distribution of nearly the same width centred on x rather than on μ. Why the Percentile CI Works puts those two distributions on one axis; CI Coverage runs many intervals and counts the hits.
