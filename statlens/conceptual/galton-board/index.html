A ball meets n pegs on the way down and goes right at each one with probability p. The bin it lands in is the number of rights — so the pile is a Binomial(n, p) distribution being drawn by hand.

  • Rows of pegs is n, the number of trials. Chance of going right is p. Changing either clears the board, because balls that fell through a different board are not the same experiment.
  • +1 and +10 animate; +100 and +1000 go straight into the bins, because a hundred balls at once is a smear rather than a demonstration. ▶ drops them in a steady stream.
  • The purple curve is the exact Binomial(n, p) probability for each bin, scaled to the number of balls dropped — so the pile growing into it is a fair comparison and not two y-axes that happen to agree.
  • The orange dashed curve is the normal approximation, mean np and SD √(npq). Turn it on at n = 4 and at n = 20 to see when it starts being worth using.

Two boards

Ideal decides each bounce with a coin and then draws the path. The counts are exactly Binomial(n, p), which is what makes it the board to point at when you mean “this is the binomial distribution”. The motion is still honest — projectile arcs, a squash at each peg — but nothing is being simulated.

Physical drops real balls into a real little world: gravity, bounces off pegs, bounces off each other, piles that rest. Nothing decides where a ball should land; it falls and ends up somewhere. The pile this produces is not binomial, and that is the point of having both. The curve stays drawn over it, scaled to the pile, and a line underneath reports how far the spread has drifted from √(npq).

The knobs in Physical mode each break a different thing the binomial model assumes:

  • Ball size and peg size — a ball too big for the gap can skip a row or ride a diagonal, so it no longer makes exactly n decisions.
  • Bounciness — a lively ball can travel sideways across several pegs, or back upward. Same violation, more obvious.
  • Balls hit each other — this is the interesting one. It breaks independence between balls, the assumption students nod past fastest. Drop them one at a time and the pile behaves; open the tap and watch it deform, on the same board with the same pegs.

Slow motion quarters the speed without changing the physics, which is the way to actually see a bounce.

Both boards are reproducible: ?seed= gives the identical run every time, including in Physical mode — the world advances in fixed steps and the balls are released against its own clock rather than yours, so a slow machine gets the same pile, just drawn less often.

For the same distribution as a calculator rather than a pile, see the Binomial Distribution Calculator. For why the normal curve shows up at all, see Sampling Distributions and the Law of Large Numbers.
