Where the Curve Comes From

A sample mean is not interesting on its own — what matters is how far it sits from μ relative to how much sample means bounce around. That distance, counted in standard errors, is the statistic. This page builds its distribution one sample at a time.

The two recipes

Both start from the same gap, x̄ − μ. They differ in what they divide it by:

  • σ/√n — the true standard error. The same number every time, because σ is a fact about the population.
  • s/√n — the standard error you can actually compute, using this sample's own s. A different number every time.

What to look for

With σ/√n the pile matches the normal curve drawn over it. Flip to s/√n — the same samples, rescored — and the pile grows a taller peak and heavier tails that spill past the curve. Nothing about x̄ changed. The extra spread comes entirely from the denominator wobbling.

That is why a t table exists. If you used 1.96 on the second pile you would reject far more often than 5% of the time — the tally under the figure counts exactly how often.

Now drag the sample size up. The bigger n is, the less s wobbles, and the two piles become hard to tell apart — which is why the bottom row of a t table is the normal row.
