    Critical Numbers

    A critical number of f is an x in the domain of f where f ′(x) = 0 or f ′(x) does not exist. Both halves matter, and the second half is the one that gets left out.

    In the domain of f

    A point where f ′ blows up is only a critical number if f itself is defined there. For f(x) = x + 1/x the derivative does not exist at 0 — but neither does f, so 0 is not a critical number, it is simply not in the domain. The page applies that test, which is why some functions come back with fewer critical numbers than the denominator suggests.

    Critical does not mean extreme

    f(x) = x³ has a critical number at 0 and no turning point there: f ′ = 3x² is zero without changing sign. The sign chart is what settles it, and the verdict beside each critical number comes from the sign on either side — the first derivative test, done for you so you can check your own.

    How the numbers are found

    Factor by factor, not by scanning the whole derivative. A linear or quadratic factor gives its roots exactly — 6/7, not 0.857 — and a factor like x² gives 0 even though f ′ never changes sign there, which a hunt for sign changes would walk straight past.

    Anything else is found numerically between −12 and 12, and the page says so rather than letting a shortened list look complete.
