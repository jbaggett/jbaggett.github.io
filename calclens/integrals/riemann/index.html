Riemann Sums

Type a function, give it an interval, and choose where each rectangle takes its height — at the left edge of its strip, the right edge, or the midpoint. The slider sets how many rectangles there are.

One rectangle is a product

This is the part worth slowing down for. Each rectangle is

a height read off the curve, times a width that every rectangle shares. If f is a speed in metres per second and x is a time in seconds, one rectangle is a distance in metres — and so is the total. The sum is the easy half; knowing what one product means is not.

Step through them with ◀ and ▶, or click a rectangle, and the line underneath spells out that one product with its numbers in.

Over or under

Where the rectangle's top sits above the curve, that strip is being over-counted; where it sits below, under-counted. For a function that only decreases, left rectangles overshoot and right rectangles fall short — so the two together trap the answer between them without your needing to know it. Midpoint rectangles overshoot on one side of their strip and fall short on the other, which is why they do so much better.

The exact area is hidden until you ask

Press R, or the button under the figure, to show it (?readout=true starts it open). Until then the page tells you the estimate and nothing else, which is the point: make the rectangles thinner and watch where the number is heading, then check.

The exact value is computed numerically, so it works for functions with no elementary antiderivative too.

Signed area

Below the axis, a rectangle's height is negative and so is its product. The page draws those hatched rather than relying on colour, and they subtract from the total. A Riemann sum computes signed area, not the area of the paint.

Putting this in a slide or a page

?f=4-x^2&a=0&b=2&rule=left&n=8&readout=true&embed=true
